## 1. Every element earns its place
Before adding anything (a cut, a camera move, an effect, a label, an icon, a sound), write its job in one line: what does
the viewer learn or feel because of it? No answer, no element. A beautiful effect with no job is noise.

## 2. Structure: scenes, not shots
- A film is 5 to 9 **scenes**. A scene is one place and one dramatic question, with a beginning (we arrive, we learn
  where we are), a middle (something changes) and an end (a result we can name). Write the scene list before any shot.
- Each scene has a **camera plan** made in advance: the opening frame, one or two motivated moves, the closing frame.
  The move follows the story: a reveal, a follow, a push toward what matters, a pull back to show consequence.
- Shots per film: about 18 to 35 for 2.5 minutes (average shot 5 to 8 s). Short shots (under 2 s) only at the climax and
  only when the story speeds up. More than 40 shots in 3 minutes is wrong for these films.

## 3. Cutting
- Cut only for a reason: new information, a change of place or time, a reaction, a change of scale (wide to close to
  explain a detail), or to compress time the story does not need. A music hit alone is never a reason; when a story beat
  falls near a strong hit, put the cut on the hit.
- Cut on action or on the end of a sentence, never in the middle of a phrase the viewer is reading.
- Hold after every key line: at least one beat (0.5 to 1.5 s) on the image that the line just explained.
- Sound leads or trails the picture across a cut (J and L cuts): the next scene's ambience starts under the last second
  of the current one.
- Transitions: a hard cut by default; a dissolve only for passing time or a change of world; a match cut or a push
  through an element (a door, a flag, smoke, a map) when two scenes are linked in meaning. Never a transition for show.

## 4. Camera and time
- Real time. Never speed up an animation or a camera move to fit the narration; change the plan instead (start earlier,
  move less distance, hold longer). A move has an ease in, a steady middle and an ease out, with speeds a real camera
  operator could make: a slow push is 5 to 10 % of frame per second, a pan crosses the frame in 4 s or more.
- One camera idea per shot. Height and angle say something: high and top-down = understanding, the map, the plan;
  low and close = stakes, scale, people.

## 5. Living worlds
Fill the frame with the life of the place, quietly: wind in grass and banners, drifting cloud shadows, smoke and dust,
water that moves, birds, flickering lamps, small figures doing things in the distance, depth haze. The foreground,
middle and background all have something. Empty does not mean calm; calm is a full frame that moves slowly.

## 6. Graphics and type
- No emoji, no cartoon icons, no faces on objects. Graphics are refined, flat, consistent: heraldic banners in each
  side's colours, simplified military unit symbols, thin ink arrows that draw on with a tapered head, small caps labels
  with a hairline leader.
- A **legend** frames the battlefield like a motion-graphics map: the two sides (colour, banner, commander, strength),
  the unit types used, and the key places (river, hill, village) introduced once, each when the narration first names
  it, and kept on screen in a corner panel while they matter.
- Type: one display face and one text face per film; labels never cover the action; nothing clips the frame edge.

## 7. Armies and action (Battle Map)
- Units are formations of many small figures or a clean formation block with rank texture, standing on the terrain,
  spaced so no two units ever overlap; when two lines meet, they meet at a front, they do not pass through each other.
- Every move is a story move: the narration names it, the arrow draws it, the units follow it.
- The decisive moment is shown twice: once wide (what happened), once closer or slower (how it worked).
- Explain like the best teacher: stakes and people first, then the plan of each side drawn on the map, then the turn,
  then the result in plain numbers, then why it mattered. Humour lives in the narration and in timing, not in clip art.

## 8. Music and sound
- Battle Map: ambient strategy-game feel (Age of Empires, Anno): warm pads, plucked and bowed strings, soft hand drums,
  a solo instrument for the place and era, long evolving chords; it swells only at the turn of the battle and settles
  after. It sits under the voice and never fights it.
- Dark Corners: restraint; silence is a tool.
- Sound effects only for things we see or feel (horns, distant drums, wind, water, a door); nothing decorative.

## 9. Checks before a render (the director runs them automatically)
Scene list with beginning, middle, end for each; shot count and average shot length inside the range above; every cut
has a written reason; no speed-up (camera speed and animation speed limits); no overlapping or clipped labels; no
emoji or cartoon icons; the legend present on Battle Map; at least three layers of ambient motion in every wide shot;
contact sheet reviewed by a separate eye (model or agent) with the narration under each still.
