## 1. Fact check of the two pilot scripts

Verdict key: OK = correct as said. S = simplify-but-true (true, but can leave a wrong picture). WRONG = false.

### 1.1 Fossil: "How does a dinosaur turn into stone?"

Core science, all high confidence unless marked:
- Most dead animals decay completely. Fossils form only in the right conditions [1][4]. Quick burial by mud or sand protects
  the remains; floodplains and arid areas are where dinosaur bones are commonly preserved [6] (high).
- Permineralisation: water carrying dissolved minerals seeps into the tiny holes in bone and the minerals crystallise; this is
  "the most common method" [1][2][3]. The result still contains part of the original animal, it is not a copy [3]. Replacement
  (the whole bone swapped for mineral) is the rarer extreme case [3].
- It takes a very long time: "millions of years" for the organic parts to break down and minerals to fill in [2][3].
- Fossils reach us because rock layers rise and wear away: "Uplift and erosion occur, bringing the fossil closer to the
  surface" [5]; at cliffs, "As the waves erode the cliffs, they crumble away and release fossils onto the beaches" [2].
- The chance of becoming a fossil is "one in a million" as a figure of speech, not a measured number [6] (medium). Do not
  put a number on screen.
- Convention: "fossil" means evidence of life more than about 12,000 years old [3] (high).

#### 1.1.1 Is "fell asleep forever" right for 6 to 8 year olds? (judgement)

What the sources say (all high confidence on the advice itself; they are written for grieving or bereaved children, so applying
them to a story about an animal 150 million years ago is my INFERENCE):
- Dougy Center (children's grief centre): "Avoid euphemisms such as passed away, went to sleep, crossed over, or lost, as they
  can confuse young children." Suggested plain wording: "her body stopped working" [13].
- Sesame Workshop psychologist Rosemarie Truglio calls "put to sleep" particularly problematic: a child may think "I go to sleep
  every night. Am I going to die?"; euphemisms "can confuse and even scare children" [14].
- Le Bonheur Children's: use "dead" and "died"; softening phrases "may confuse your child and potentially cause additional
  fears" [15]. Children's Mercy: "If you describe death as somebody being asleep, your child will be afraid to go to sleep"
  and "Avoid using euphemisms" [16]. Ele's Place: euphemisms like "sleeping" "can be confusing", and "Children need to know
  that death is permanent" [17].
- Developmental point: children typically grasp that death is irreversible, final, universal and has a cause between about 5 and
  7, but vary a lot [18]. So 6 to 8 year olds can hold "it died", and "asleep forever" even clashes with what they already know.
- Fear research: for the 6 to 8 range appearance matters less and understanding fantasy versus real is developing; realistic
  harm to people like them frightens older children most [20] (medium; the article groups preschool 2 to 7 and older 8 to 12,
  so 6 to 8 is straddling). A dinosaur dying quietly of old age by a river, with no injury, no predator and no people, is the
  mildest honest version.
- A counter-view to flag: these sources address a child facing a real death. A storybook about deep time is lower stakes, and
  "fell asleep" is a very common children's book device. My judgement: do not use it, because the only cost of "died" is one
  calm beat, and the cost of "asleep" is a known bedtime fear plus a dishonest line in a series whose promise is
  "facts checked". Keep the feeling soft through picture and music (slow, warm, no sad sting), not through a euphemism.
- Two other honest options if Sam wants it even softer: "its long life was over" (still clear, no sleep), or "one day, very
  old, it died, as every living thing does one day" (universality, but adds words). I recommend "very old and tired, it lay
  down and died".
- Not sourced (INFERENCE): most fossil dinosaurs did not die peacefully of old age. The film shows one imagined animal ("a
  dinosaur lived right here"). That is fine as a story; just never claim this is how a particular fossil died.

Words 6 to 8 may not know: fossil (taught on screen), ferns, layer, millions (as a size), seeped, "poking out". Frightening: only
line 4. Confusing: line 2 and 10 (whole dinosaur versus bone), line 1 (alive rock).

#### 1.1.2 Recommended fossil script (124 words)

1. This stone was once a dinosaur's bone.
2. How does a dinosaur bone turn into stone?
3. Long ago, a big, gentle dinosaur lived right here, munching ferns by the river.
4. One day, very old and tired, it lay down and died.
5. The river brought mud and sand, which covered its bones like a blanket.
6. More mud came. And more. Layer on layer, for millions of years.
7. Deep down, water seeped into the bones and left tiny bits of stone. Slowly, they turned to rock.
8. Then wind and rain slowly wore the land away.
9. One sunny day, a girl with a brush spotted something poking out of the rock.
10. A fossil! A real dinosaur bone, turned to stone.
11. I wonder what is waiting under your feet.

### 1.2 Volcano: "How is a volcano born?"

Core science:
- Magma is molten rock underground; lava is molten rock that has broken through to the surface; lava starts red hot and
  darkens and hardens as it cools [21] (high).
- Magma rises mainly because it is less dense than the surrounding rock [22] (medium: Wikipedia, but the statement is textbook
  and consistent with [25]). Magma also rises through cracks [22].
- Pressure and gas: magma holds dissolved gas under high pressure; as it rises the pressure drops, gas comes out as bubbles
  and pushes the magma out. USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory compares this to a shaken soda can; slow gas escape gives a gentle
  eruption, fast gas escape an explosive one [23] (high). This also means "bursts" is only half the story: many eruptions
  flow gently [23].
- Rumbling: rising magma breaks rock and causes small earthquakes, ground swelling and more gas, which are the warning signs
  [24] (high, quoting a USGS volcanologist).
- Most of the Earth's interior is NOT molten; pressure usually prevents melting. Magma forms only in limited places (where
  plates pull apart, or where water lowers the melting point at subduction zones), and magma chambers are mostly solid, like a
  wet sponge [25] (medium-high). So "so hot that rock can melt" is true but should say "in some places".
- Composite volcanoes (stratovolcanoes) are "steep-sided conical mountains composed of alternating layers of lava and ash" [26]
  (medium-high; this is a university seismology site). Not every volcano grows this way (INFERENCE: shield volcanoes are
  broad, and big explosions can lower a summit; I did not open a source for those two points).
- Volcanic soil is fertile: soils around volcanoes are nutrient-rich and renewed by ash falls [27] (high, NASA Earthdata);
  weathering of ash and basalt releases phosphorus, calcium, iron, magnesium and potassium [28] (medium, WorldAtlas).
- But plants come back slowly. On Kilauea Iki, algae arrived first, then lichen, then mosses and ferns; nine years after the
  eruption the surface "looked practically barren"; soil forms only as the rock weathers; full forest recovery can take
  hundreds of thousands of years [29] (medium-high, journalism quoting the botanists' study). So "rain and sun make the land
  green again" is too quick.
- Hawaii: a chain of over 80 volcanoes built over a hot spot as the Pacific Plate moves roughly 2 to 4 inches a year; "each
  eruption adds land"; Lōʻihi is a growing volcano still under the ocean that "may become the next island"; Mauna Loa rises
  "more than 30,000 feet from the bottom of the sea" [30] (high, US National Park Service).
- A real "birth": in 1943 the volcano Paricutin emerged in a farmer's cornfield in Mexico and reached 336 m in its first year;
  it stopped in 1952 at 424 m [31] (high, ESA).

#### 1.2.1 Recommended volcano script (125 words)

1. Far below us, it is so hot that rock can melt.
2. How is a volcano born?
3. Melted rock is called magma. It is lighter than the rock around it, so it rises.
4. It finds a crack. Gas bubbles push, like a shaken fizzy drink. The ground rumbles.
5. Whoosh! It bursts out of the top. Now we call it lava.
6. The lava cools, and turns into brand new rock.
7. Each eruption adds a layer, and the mountain grows.
8. When the volcano rests, plants find a home on the new rock, and slowly it turns to rich soil.
9. The islands of Hawaii were built this way, one eruption at a time.
10. Our planet is always making new land, and the ground under your feet is slowly moving.

Optional "wow" beat if Sam wants a real birth story: "In 1943, a farmer in Mexico watched a volcano be born in his
cornfield." Paricutin reached 336 m in its first year [31]. Avoid size comparisons (skyscrapers, houses): none is sourced.
If a number is shown, set it in code as a label: 336 m.

## 2. Four new scripts (each fact-checked)

Shape: line 1 hook, line 2 the question, one idea per line, last line sends the child back to their world.

### 2.1 under-your-feet: "Who lives under your feet?" (121 words)

1. Shh. Listen. Something is wriggling under the grass.
2. Who lives under your feet?
3. Worms! Their tunnels let air and water reach the roots of plants.
4. Some ants build underground towns, with a nursery for the babies.
5. A mole swims through the soil with its big paws, hunting for worms.
6. Rabbits dig a maze of tunnels called a warren, with cosy rooms for their babies.
7. A mother fox raises her pups in a den, safe and snug.
8. And far below, a baby cicada sips juice from tree roots, and waits.
9. Some cicadas wait for seventeen years!
10. Then, all together, they climb out into the sun.
11. So next time you sit on the grass, say hello. A busy world is right below you.

Fact check:
- L3 worms: tunnelling lets soil "drain water, bring in oxygen, and create space for plant roots" [32] (high). A good garden acre
  may hold about a million earthworms [32] (medium). Do NOT say "five hearts": the source says five aortic arches that work as
  hearts, a technicality [33] (medium); skip.
- L4 ants: some ant nests reach "as far as 25 feet underground" with chambers for the queen, nurseries, fungus farms and trash
  dumps [34] (high for that study's species; hence "Some ants"). Not all ants nest in soil (INFERENCE).
- L5 mole: digs roughly 20 metres of tunnel a day, pushing soil with its forepaws "much as a swimmer moves"; earthworms are the
  favourite food; very small eyes [35] (high).
- L6 rabbits: a warren is a network of tunnels dug mostly by females; does give birth underground [36] (medium-high; Country
  Life). "Maze" is mine, kid picture only.
- L7 fox: red foxes raise their young in an underground burrow called a den; pups arrive March and April, family moves later
  [37] (high, Illinois DNR). Source says nothing on foxes digging their own; the line avoids it.
- L8 and L9 cicadas: periodical cicada nymphs feed on tree root fluid for 13 or 17 years [38][39] (high); "Annual cicadas take
  about one year" [38]. Hence "some cicadas".
- L10: periodical cicadas "stay belowground for a fixed amount of time" and then "emerge together" [39]; trigger is soil warmth,
  about 18 C at 8 inches (20 cm) [38] (high). No claim about singing made, because I did not source it.
- Amazing true fact alternatives (sourced): one tablespoon of healthy soil holds more organisms than there are people on Earth
  (FAO 2015 fact sheet title) [40] (medium: I only saw the title, not the body). Cicadas at 1.5 million per acre in 2016 [38]
  (medium, a newspaper figure quoted by Live Science).
- Words 6 to 8 may not know: warren, den, pups, cicada, nursery. All explained by picture. Nothing frightening (watch that
  the fox is a mother, not a hunter: no predation shown). Possible confusion: scale. Show the block with the roots as the
  shared thread. Weak point: this is a tour, not a story; capacity research says children follow narrative first [65]. The
  cicada thread (waiting, then climbing out) is the story; keep it as the spine.

### 2.2 rain: "Where does rain come from?" (125 words)

1. Look up! That cloud is made of water.
2. Where does rain come from?
3. The sun warms the sea, and some water rises into the air as a gas you cannot see.
4. Up high, it is cold, and the invisible water turns back into tiny drops.
5. Lots of tiny drops together make a cloud.
6. The drops bump and stick and grow. It takes about a million to make one raindrop!
7. Now it is too heavy to float. It falls. That is rain!
8. The rain fills the rivers, the rivers run to the sea, and the sun starts all over again.
9. Round and round it goes, with no beginning and no end.
10. So when rain taps your window, say hello. It may have started in the sea.

Fact check:
- Sun drives the cycle; heat turns water to vapor; air holds invisible water vapor [41][42] (high).
- Warm vapor-filled air rises into colder air and condenses into tiny liquid droplets; droplets combine into heavier drops and
  it can rain [41] (high). Clouds are "mostly" water drops (ice at height; INFERENCE, kid film ignores it).
- About a million cloud droplets make one raindrop: a typical raindrop about 2 mm across holds roughly a million cloud droplets
  [44] (medium-high); another textbook gives the same order of magnitude [43] (medium). My own volume check: a 2 mm drop is
  about a million times a 20 micrometre droplet and about 8 million times a 10 micrometre one, so "about a million" is an
  order-of-magnitude figure. Keep "about".
- Cloud droplets float because air friction and small updrafts keep them suspended; raindrops fall [43] (high). "Too heavy to
  float" is the kid version.
- Over 96 percent of Earth's water is in the oceans [41] (high), so "started in the sea" is likely but not certain: "may".
- "Always happening, no single starting point" [41] (high).
- DO NOT use the popular "you drink water dinosaurs drank": the page that makes the claim gives no evidence [45]. I found no
  source I could open that supports it, so it is out.
- Words 6 to 8 may not know: "gas" (known as a word, unclear as an idea), "invisible". Use "water vapor" as an on-screen label only
  if Sam wants one new word. Nothing frightening. Confusing: line 3 (water you cannot see) is the hardest idea; give it a clear
  picture of shimmering air over the sea. The film is a journey (a story), which fits the narrative-first finding [65].

### 2.3 deep-sea: "What lives in the deep sea?" (120 words)

1. It is dark. It is cold. And something is glowing.
2. What lives in the deep sea?
3. Near the top is the sunlight zone, bright and busy with fish.
4. Deeper is the twilight zone, where the light fades away.
5. Deeper still is the midnight zone. No sunlight reaches it.
6. But it is not empty! Many animals there make their own light.
7. Some anglerfish dangle a glowing lantern, to invite their dinner closer.
8. Around hot vents on the seafloor, giant tube worms live without any sunlight.
9. And the biggest secret? We have seen only a tiny piece of the deep seafloor.
10. Who knows what is waiting down there?
11. Next time you see the sea, imagine all the little lights, twinkling far below.

Fact check:
- Zones: sunlight (epipelagic) 0 to 200 m, "most visible light"; twilight (mesopelagic) 200 to 1,000 m, light very faint,
  animals often have bioluminescence and large eyes; midnight (bathypelagic) 1,000 to 4,000 m, "constant darkness", only light
  is animals' own, about 4 C [46] (high). NOAA Ocean Exploration uses the same boundaries and calls the deeper layers abyss
  (4,000 to 6,000 m) and hadal (below 6,000 m) [47] (high).
- Glow: MBARI says 76 percent of creatures in the water column and 45 percent of those on the bottom can make their own light,
  from observations with remotely operated vehicles between the surface and 4,000 m [48] (high). Study detail: Martini and
  Haddock, Scientific Reports 2017, 240 dives in the Pacific, more than 500 types, more than 350,000 sightings [49] (high).
  Careful wording: it is about animals the cameras saw, not every species [48]. The line says "Many animals", which is safe. If
  Sam wants the number: "In one big study, about three animals out of four could make their own light." (MBARI wording [48]).
- Anglerfish: only some have a lure (esca); only females; the glow comes from glowing bacteria living in the lure; scientists do
  not yet know how the fish get their bacteria [50] (high). Live in roughly 300 to 5,000 m [51] (medium-high). Hence "Some
  anglerfish". Do not say the fish makes the light itself.
- Vents: first found near the Galapagos Rift in 1977; vent animals do not depend on sunlight, bacteria and archaea use
  chemosynthesis to get energy from chemicals; giant tube worms can reach almost five feet in less than two years [52] (high).
- "Seen only a tiny piece": a 2025 Science Advances study (Ocean Discovery League, lead author Katy Croff Bell) estimates
  humans have visually observed less than 0.001 percent of the deep seafloor, roughly the size of Rhode Island; "deep" there
  means below 200 m [53] (medium-high: reported by Ocean News, not the paper itself).
  Do NOT merge with "mapped": a different figure. Seabed 2030 says 28.7 percent of the ocean floor is now mapped (announced 20
  April 2026) [54] (medium). Mapping by sonar is not seeing. Do not say "only 5 percent explored" (a popular figure I could not
  confirm to a source I opened). If Sam wants a number on screen: "Less than 0.001%" with the label "of the deep seafloor has
  been seen by people" (Bell et al. 2025 via [53]).
- Words 6 to 8 may not know: zone, vents, tube worms, lantern. Drop "bioluminescence" (too long); say "make their own light".
  Frightening: deep-sea animals can look scary to 3 to 5 year olds (appearance drives fear at that age [20]); 6 to 8 is
  transitional. Keep anglerfish teeth soft, show the lure, not a bite (INFERENCE). Confusing: three zones in three lines is
  dense; use the cut-open block's depth bands as the picture.

### 2.4 shooting-star: "What is a shooting star?" (122 words)

1. Look! A bright streak of light, zooming across the night sky.
2. What is a shooting star?
3. It is not a star at all. Stars are giant, glowing balls of gas, very far away.
4. It is a tiny piece of space rock, usually no bigger than a pebble.
5. It zooms into our air, very, very fast.
6. The air rubs against it, and it gets so hot that it glows.
7. Whoosh! A few seconds of light, high above the clouds, and the rock is gone.
8. So no star fell at all.
9. Sometimes a bigger rock survives and lands. Then it is called a meteorite.
10. Tonight, if the sky is dark and clear, look up and wait.
11. A tiny visitor from space might say hello.

Fact check:
- A star is a huge glowing ball of very hot gas; the Sun is the nearest, the next, Proxima Centauri, about 4.3 light-years away
  [61] (high).
- Meteoroid: a piece of rock or metal in space; meteor: the streak of light when it enters the atmosphere, usually only a few
  seconds; "shooting stars" is the popular name; meteorite: the part that reaches the ground [55][56] (high).
- Size: NASA says typically "pebble-sized, but could be a little smaller or a little larger" [56]; Texas Parks and Wildlife says
  most are "no larger than grains of sand" [57]; Wikipedia gives 1 mm or smaller for most (no inline citation on that
  sentence) [59]. The sources differ, so "usually no bigger than a pebble" (true under both) is the safe line. CONFLICT noted.
- Height and heat: most burn up "while still thirty to fifty miles above the earth" [57]; other sources give about 50 to 95 km
  [59] (consistent: roughly 50 to 100 km, far above clouds). The glow is from friction/heating: friction with the atmosphere
  vaporises the dust and ionises nearby atoms [60] (high). "The air rubs against it" is the kid version; strictly, compression
  of air in front of it does most of the heating (INFERENCE, I did not open a page on that), so this is S, not wrong.
- Speed: "very fast" only. Numbers vary by source (25,000 to 160,000 mph [58]; one Perseid over 200,000 km/h [60]).
- Number: "as many as 25 million meteors may arrive daily" [58] (low-medium); NASA StarChild says "about 3,000 metric tons"
  of space dust falls daily [55] (medium, but other pages quote different tonnages). Use neither number in narration.
- Where meteors come from: Perseid dust comes from comet Swift-Tuttle [60] (high). A possible bonus line for a later version.
- Folklore ("make a wish") is tradition; if used, say "people say" (INFERENCE, not a fact claim).

Dinosaur impact, recommendation: LEAVE IT OUT of this film. Reasons:
1. The film's comfort is that a shooting star is a tiny, harmless pebble; linking it to the impact in the same minute
   contradicts that in a child's head (INFERENCE).
2. The impact is a mass death: the asteroid was about 7 miles (12 km) wide, hit about 66 million years ago, and the chain of
   events ended the non-bird dinosaurs and about 75 percent of species [62] (high for the numbers). Death of a whole group is
   the heaviest topic in the series; the bereavement guidance says to be plain, not to hide [13][15], but a one-line mention
   in a film about something else cannot be plain and gentle at once.
3. If Sam wants it, make it its own film (suggested: "Where did the big dinosaurs go?") with a gentle, honest shape: a very big
   space rock hit the Earth long ago; the world changed; the big dinosaurs could not live in the new world; some small feathered
   dinosaurs did, and today we call them birds. The "some birds survived" part is supported [62]; the line "birds are
   dinosaurs" is standard science but I did not open a page for it, so confirm before voicing (medium).

Words 6 to 8 may not know: meteorite (taught on screen, once), pebble is known. Avoid "meteoroid". Frightening: none. Confusing:
"star that is not a star" is the hook; line 3 must come quickly.

## 4. Kids craft (dense)

### 4.1 What holds 6 to 8 year olds in 60 s

- Pace. About 124 words per minute is cited as a processing limit for young children against 160 to 170 for adults, and Fred
  Rogers is said to have spoken at about 124 [66] (medium-low: a university news item quoting an audiologist, not a study I
  could open). Audiobook and podcast narrators run 150 to 160 [67] (medium). The brief's 110 to 125 words in 60 s, with
  pauses, fits the child figure. The pilots are 137 and 141 words, about 10 to 13 percent over 125; keep to 125.
  Recommendation: write for ~2 words a second including pauses; leave one beat of silence after the question and after each
  reveal (INFERENCE).
- Sentence length. Average of 9 to 12 words, longest 18, one idea per line (my scripts above). Listening comprehension runs
  ahead of reading comprehension from early childhood through at least middle school [68] (high), so spoken words can be a
  little richer than a 6 year old can read, if the picture carries them (INFERENCE from [68]). Teach at most one or two new
  words per film and show them as large labels (already in the README grammar).
- Repetition. Blue's Clues repeats key words and phrases (one host said "predict" about 15 times in an episode) and minimises
  editing and montage so children "spend the work on the content" [63] (high). In 60 s, repeat ONE word (e.g., "layer on
  layer", "deeper") and the film's question as the final echo.
- Meaning first. When researchers played Sesame Street dialogue in Greek, children watched far less: "meaning matters" [64].
  Fast and slow versions of Sesame Street did not change attention afterwards in that team's test [64]. A separate study of 4
  year olds found 9 minutes of a very fast cartoon left executive function worse than a slower show, but it measured only
  immediate effects, at age 4, and the authors offered fast pacing or fantastical content as possible causes [69] (medium).
  No directly comparable data for 6 to 8 was found. Our fixed view and slow pace are on the safe side either way.
- Story first. Fisch's capacity model: children give working memory to the narrative first; educational content is understood
  largely through narrative comprehension; "educational content must be well embedded into the narrative"; prior knowledge
  helps children keep new ideas from one viewing [65] (high; tested on preschoolers). Scripts that tell a journey (the
  dinosaur's story, the raindrop's trip, the magma's climb) beat lists. Make the "tour" scripts (under your feet, deep sea)
  carry a thread (the cicada's wait; going down a staircase of light).
- Music and sound effects. Bluey's composer: "creating clarity above everything else", a handmade feel, nothing loud or
  obnoxious, an "internal" approach that shows the kids' view instead of telling the audience what to think [73][74] (high that
  he said it). Hey Bear Sensory uses family dance music (house, disco, hip hop) rather than nursery rhymes and tells emotion
  through movement with no dialogue; each video takes four to five months [76] (medium-high; baby and toddler audience, not 6
  to 8). I found NO research on sound effects for this age, so effects stay a craft choice: put one on each reveal, always
  below the voice (INFERENCE).
- Humour. Operation Ouch is a "comedy children's television series" with hands-on experiments by two doctors [72] (medium);
  Bluey draws humour from real play and parenting for adults too, and treats children as "intelligent, capable people" [75]
  (low: the page flags its own inconsistencies). Nat Geo Kids sells facts as "weird" and uses question teasers [77] (high).
  For 60 s: one small joke per film, visual (a worm that waves), never sarcasm (INFERENCE).
- Recurring character. SciShow Kids uses a host plus Squeaks, a robot rat who plays the curious learner and asks kids' questions
  [71] (medium-high). Children form one-sided bonds with favourite characters, and attention to a character can beat attention
  to a nearby adult, but the small Dora study (50 children, age 4 to 5) did not show better learning [70] (medium; evidence
  for learning gains is thin). INFERENCE for the series: a recurring tiny visual creature in the corner of every block (a snail
  or a ladybird) who reacts, never speaks, gives a "find it" game and a rewatch reason, costs nothing in words, and does not
  break the fixed-view grammar. The narrator remains the series' constant voice.
- Structure. YouTube's own kids' guidance asks for "a complete narrative" with a clear takeaway, and warns against videos without
  a clear beginning, middle and end, or with unclear audio [82] (high). The hook, question, journey, wonder shape meets it.

### 4.2 What the best kids' channels and shows do (as far as I could verify)

- SciShow Kids: opens with a specific "Why?" or "How?" question, framed as a mystery, real footage next to clean animation, a
  host who translates a robot rat's squeaks [71].
- Operation Ouch: twin doctors, experiments on the body, "try this at home", 28 minute episodes [72]; model for humour and
  hands-on, not for 60 s.
- Nat Geo Kids Weird But True: curiosity teasers, short videos, hands-on "make" projects [77][78]; I could not open any page
  with video length or YouTube pacing, so no claim on that.
- Hey Bear Sensory (babies and toddlers): high realism of the fruit so children can find it in real life, positive tone,
  non-nursery music, no dialogue [76]. Lesson for us: music can carry the series; realism of the thing named helps recognition.
- Bluey: about 7 minute episodes, based on real child play, interruptions and topic changes, small details, resolutions that
  favour resilience over neat endings [75] (low, secondary); score by clarity and a handmade sound [73][74].

### 4.3 YouTube Made for Kids (MFK): rules that affect these films

Sources: YouTube Help pages [79][80][81][82][83][84][85]; FTC action [86]; COPPA rule update [87].

- Designation is YOUR legal responsibility. "Regardless of your location, you're legally required to comply with COPPA"; YouTube's
  machine learning may flag content but "Do not rely on our systems to set your audience for you"; setting can be channel level
  (every video) or video level; wrong settings can bring "compliance issues with the FTC or other authorities" and action on your
  account [79] (high). LAWYER: whether a mixed-audience science channel counts as MFK is a legal judgement. YouTube's own
  criteria lean toward MFK for these films: educational content for young children, language meant for children, simple
  songs or stories, child-appealing animated characters [81] (high). A film made for 6 to 8 year olds is MFK on those
  criteria in my reading; get a lawyer's view before treating any film as "general audience" (US law applies to a Canadian
  creator per YouTube's page [79]; Canadian rules were not researched).
- Enforcement is real: in Sept 2025 Disney agreed to a $10 million civil penalty over allegations (not findings) that it
  failed to designate child-directed videos as MFK; the order includes a 10 year audience-designation programme [86] (high).
- COPPA Rule amendments: published 22 April 2025, effective 23 June 2025, compliance by 22 April 2026 [87] (high). Mostly
  about data handling; relevance to a creator is indirect (INFERENCE). LAWYER if Sam ever collects data from kids (email
  lists, quizzes) on his own site.
- Features off on MFK videos: autoplay on home, cards or end screens, video watermarks, channel memberships, comments, donate
  button, live chat, merchandise and ticketing, notification bell, personalised advertising, miniplayer, Super Chat, save to
  playlist or Watch Later [79][80]. Whole MFK channels also lose memberships, notification bell, posts and comments [79][80].
  Consequences: no end-screen link to the next film (build series playlists and rely on suggested videos; I did not find
  confirmation that creator playlists still work, check in Studio); no watermark; no comments to moderate.
- Money: "we don't serve personalized ads on kids content", which "may result in a decrease in revenue" [79] (high).
  MFK videos are "more likely to be recommended alongside other kids' videos" [79]. Matches the README: brand, books, prints
  off-platform. LAWYER or ACCOUNTANT: sponsorship, book and print income structure and taxes are not covered here.
- Content quality rules (YouTube kids and family principles). Good: "age-appropriate, enriching, engaging, and inspiring",
  complete narrative, critical thinking [82][83]. Avoid: heavy brand or product focus, "deceptively educational" titles or
  thumbnails, untrue or exaggerated content, keyword stuffing, dangerous activities, confusing videos with no clear
  beginning, middle and end or unclear audio, strange use of well-known children's characters [82][83]. Low quality: "may get
  limited or no ads", and a channel "may be suspended from the YouTube Partner Program" [82]. Not "kids content" by YouTube's
  rules: mature themes (sex, violence), age-restricted videos [81].
- "Made for kids" does not mean YouTube treats it as appropriate: "We don't automatically consider all 'made for kids' content
  appropriate for kids" [80].
- What must not appear (INFERENCE from the above, check in Studio): no calls to buy or visit outside links as a kids' call to
  action; no clickbait titles; no fear thumbnails; no unsourced "facts". Dinosaur death told plainly is not a mature theme
  by those criteria, but it is the line to watch.
- AI disclosure (YouTube altered or synthetic content): disclosure is needed for realistic content; not for clearly unrealistic
  or animated content, nor for scripts, outlines or captions made with AI; but "Creates music that's the main focus of the
  video" is on the disclosure list [85] (high). The series' music is generated by Sam's score engine from sampled instruments
  (code, no AI model that I know of; not verified). Since "music at the centre" is the series' point, label the music honestly
  in the description either way (house rule: honesty, AI content labelled where platforms ask). Sam to decide.
- Repetitive content policy (monetisation): "mass-produced, generic, repetitive" is not allowed; templated AI content with a
  mass-produced impression is not; same intro and outro are fine if the bulk is different; series with "a distinct storyline,
  focus, or concept" per video are fine [84] (high). A series with a fixed view and a fixed structure is legitimate only if each
  film has its own story, facts and music variation. Keep that true.
- Needs Sam to confirm in YouTube Studio: set the channel as MFK at channel level (one setting for the whole series).
